Psychodynamic Social
Work Practice

A 14-Week Syllabus for Clinical Social Workers

Curated by Topher Rasmussen, MSW

I designed this course during my MSW at the University of Utah College of Social Work as an independent study with Dr. Joanna Bettmann Schaefer in Spring 2024. I wanted something that didn't exist in our program—a deep, sustained immersion in psychodynamic theory as it actually applies to clinical social work practice.

The course traces the evolution of psychodynamic thought from Freud through contemporary relational and intersubjective theories, always with an eye toward what this means for the clinician sitting across from a client. Special attention is given to the intersection of race, class, gender, culture, and biology with inner psychological life—the biopsychosocial person-in-environment perspective that defines our profession.

This syllabus is designed for pre-licensed social workers who want to deepen their psychodynamic understanding in the early years of practice.

Texts

  • Required Berzoff, J., Flanagan, L. M. & Hertz, P. (Eds.). (2021). Inside Out and Outside In: Psychodynamic Clinical Theory and Psychopathology in Contemporary Multicultural Contexts (5th ed.). Rowman & Littlefield.
  • Recommended McWilliams, N. (2004). Psychoanalytic Psychotherapy: A Practitioner's Guide. Guilford Press.
  • Recommended Quatman, T. (2015). Essential Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: An Acquired Art. Routledge.

Weekly Syllabus

Week 1 What Is Psychodynamic Psychotherapy, and Is It Evidence-Based?

Before we get into the theories themselves, we need to answer the question that every social worker gets asked: does this stuff actually work? Shedler's 2010 paper is the single best argument for psychodynamic therapy's empirical standing, and the Berzoff chapter grounds us in why a biopsychosocial lens matters in the first place.

Required Readings

  • Berzoff, J., Flanagan, L. M. & Hertz, P. — Ch. 1: Why a Biopsychosocial Context? Inside Out and Outside In. (13 pgs) request
  • Shedler, J. (2010). The efficacy of psychodynamic psychotherapy. American Psychologist, 65, 98–109. (11 pgs) request

Supplemental Readings

  • Fonagy, P. (2015). The effectiveness of psychodynamic psychotherapies: An update. World Psychiatry, 14(2), 137–150. (13 pgs)
  • Leichsenring, F., et al. (2023). The status of psychodynamic psychotherapy as an empirically supported treatment for common mental disorders. World Psychiatry, 22(2), 286–304. (18 pgs)
  • Midgley, N., et al. (2021). The evidence-base for psychodynamic psychotherapy with children and adolescents: A narrative synthesis. Frontiers in Psychology, 12, 662671. (18 pgs)
  • Shedler, J. (2006). That was then, this is now: Psychoanalytic psychotherapy for the rest of us. (60 pgs)

Week 2 Origins of Psychotherapy — Freud

Love him or not, you have to understand Freud to understand everything that came after. This week covers drive theory, the unconscious, and the basic architecture of psychoanalytic thought. Freud's 1914 paper on remembering, repeating, and working through remains one of the most clinically useful things he ever wrote.

Required Readings

  • Berzoff, J., Flanagan, L. M. & Hertz, P. — Ch. 2: Drive and Beyond: Freud's Psychoanalytic Concepts. Inside Out and Outside In. (32 pgs) request
  • McWilliams, N. — Ch. 1: What Defines a Psychoanalytic Therapy? Psychoanalytic Psychotherapy. (17 pgs) request
  • Freud, S. (1914). Remembering, repeating, and working through. SE 12. (6 pgs) request

Supplemental Readings

  • Coltart, N. (1993). Ch. 2: Psychoanalysis vs. psychotherapy? In How To Survive as a Psychotherapist. (12 pgs)
  • Scull, A. (2019). Ch. 6: Burying Freud. In Psychiatry and its Discontents. (13 pgs)

Week 3 Clinical Application — Transference and Countertransference

This is where theory meets the room. Transference and countertransference are the engine of psychodynamic work, and this week brings together four essential perspectives—Joseph on the total situation, Loewald on the mutuality of the process, Ogden on the first meeting, and Winnicott's deceptively brief paper that reframes the whole concept.

Required Readings

  • Joseph, B. (1985). Transference: The total situation. Int. J. Psychoanal., 66, 447–454. (8 pgs) request
  • Loewald, H. (1986). Transference–countertransference. J. Amer. Psychoanal. Assn., 34, 275–287. (13 pgs) request
  • Ogden, T. (1992). Comments on transference and countertransference in the initial analytic meeting. Psychoanal. Inquiry, 12, 225–247. (23 pgs) request
  • Winnicott, D. W. (1956). On transference. Int. J. Psychoanal., 37, 386–388. (3 pgs) request

Supplemental Readings

  • Parth, K., et al. (2017). Transference and countertransference: A review. Bull. Menninger Clinic, 81(2), 167–211. (46 pgs)
  • Pinsky, E. (2014). The potion: Reflections on Freud's "Observations of transference love." J. Amer. Psychoanal. Assn., 62, 455–474. (20 pgs)
  • Hoffman, I. Z. (1983). The patient as interpreter of the analyst's experience. Contemp. Psychoanal., 19, 389–422. (18 pgs)

Week 4 Ego Psychology

Ego psychology bridges Freud's structural model with the question social workers actually care about: how do people adapt? Anna Freud and the ego psychologists shifted attention from the id to the ego's capacity for defense, adaptation, and resilience—concepts that map directly onto strengths-based practice.

Required Readings

  • Berzoff, J., Flanagan, L. M. & Hertz, P. — Ch. 4: Ego Psychology. Inside Out and Outside In. (28 pgs) request
  • Freud, A. (1952). The mutual influences in the development of ego and id. Psychoanal. Study of the Child, 7, 42–50. (9 pgs) request

Supplemental Readings

  • Danzer, G. (2012). Integrating ego psychology and strengths-based social work. J. Theory Construction & Testing, 16, 9. (8 pgs)
  • Freud, S. (1936). Inhibitions, symptoms and anxiety. Psychoanal. Quarterly, 4, 616–625. (11 pgs)
  • Kris, E. (1951). Ego psychology and interpretation in psychoanalytic therapy. Psychoanal. Quarterly, 20, 15–30. (15 pgs)

Week 5 Race and Racism in Psychodynamic Theory

Psychodynamic theory has a complicated relationship with race. This week confronts that head-on—examining how racism operates in the consulting room, in countertransference, and within the psychoanalytic tradition itself. If psychodynamic social work is going to remain relevant, it has to reckon with who it was built by and who it has historically excluded.

Required Readings

  • Shah, N. (2022). Ch. 2: Racism. In The Analyst's Torment: Unbearable Mental States in Countertransference. (23 pgs) request
  • Akhtar, S. Xenophobia. In The Book of Emotions. request

Supplemental Readings

  • Berzoff, J., Flanagan, L. M. & Hertz, P. — Ch. 11: Psychodynamic Contributions to Understanding Racism. Inside Out and Outside In. (15 pgs)
  • Manning, M. C., et al. (2004). Empowering African Americans through social work practice. Families in Society, 85(2), 229–235. (6 pgs)
  • APsaA. Ch. 6: The Experience of Race on the Couch. Holmes Commission Final Report. (excerpt)

Week 6 Object Relations — Melanie Klein

Klein is dense, strange, and brilliant. Her ideas about the paranoid-schizoid and depressive positions, about envy and gratitude, about the way we split the world into good and bad before we can hold it as whole—these are some of the most clinically generative concepts in all of psychodynamic theory. Give her time.

Required Readings

  • Berzoff, J., Flanagan, L. M. & Hertz, P. — Ch. 5: Object Relations Theory. Inside Out and Outside In. (32 pgs) request
  • Klein, M. (1940). Mourning and its relation to manic-depressive states. Int. J. Psycho-Anal., 21, 125–153. (29 pgs) request
  • Klein, M. (1946). Notes on some schizoid mechanisms. Int. J. Psychoanal., 27, 99–110. (13 pgs) request

Supplemental Readings

  • Steiner, J. (1987). The interplay between pathological organizations and the paranoid-schizoid and depressive positions. Int. J. Psycho-Anal., 68, 69–80. (12 pgs)
  • Ogden, T. H. (1992). The contributions of Klein and Winnicott. Int. J. Psychoanal., 73, 613–626. (13 pgs)

Week 7 Clinical Application — Projection, Splitting, and Projective Identification

If Klein gave us the theory, this week is about what it looks like in practice. Ogden's paper on projective identification is one of the clearest explanations of a concept that clinicians constantly use but rarely define well. McWilliams grounds it in the language of defense mechanisms that you'll actually recognize from your caseload.

Required Readings

  • McWilliams, N. (2011). Selections from Ch. 5: Primary defensive processes. Psychoanalytic Diagnosis. (8 pgs) request
  • Ogden, T. (1979). On projective identification. Int. J. Psychoanal., 60, 357–383. (14 pgs) request

Supplemental Readings

  • Blass, R. B. (2015). Conceptualizing splitting. Int. J. Psychoanal., 96, 123–139. (17 pgs)
  • Kernberg, O. F. (1987). Projection and projective identification. J. Amer. Psychoanal. Assn., 35, 795–819. (25 pgs)

Week 8 Object Relations — Donald Woods Winnicott

Winnicott is the theorist who makes social workers feel seen. The good-enough mother, the holding environment, the capacity to be alone in the presence of another—his ideas are deeply humane and immediately applicable to clinical work. Ogden's essay on reading Winnicott is itself a masterclass in how to think psychodynamically.

Required Readings

  • Winnicott, D. W. (1949). Hate in the countertransference. Int. J. Psychoanal., 30, 69–74. (5 pgs) request
  • Ogden, T. H. (2001). Reading Winnicott. Psychoanal. Quarterly, 70(2), 299–323. (25 pgs) request

Supplemental Readings

Week 9 Clinical Application — The Frame

The frame is what makes therapy therapy. This week examines what the frame is, why it matters, and what happens when it bends or breaks—which, in social work settings, it does constantly. McWilliams and Busch provide the practical grounding, while Bleger and Bass push into the deeper meaning of the frame itself.

Required Readings

  • McWilliams, N. — Ch. 5: Boundaries I: The Frame. Psychoanalytic Psychotherapy. (21 pgs) request
  • Busch, F. (1995). Beginning a psychoanalytic treatment: Establishing an analytic frame. J. Amer. Psychoanal. Assn., 43, 449–468. (20 pgs) request

Supplemental Readings

  • Bleger, J. (1967). Psycho-analysis of the psycho-analytic frame. Int. J. Psychoanal., 48, 511–519. (8 pgs)
  • Freud, S. (1913). On beginning the treatment. Standard Edition, Vol. XII. Hogarth Press.
  • Bass, A. (2007). When the frame doesn't fit the picture. Psychoanal. Dialogues, 17(1), 1–27. (27 pgs)

Week 10 Self Psychology

Kohut's self psychology recenters the therapeutic relationship around empathy, attunement, and the selfobject experience. For social workers, this is often the most intuitive theoretical home—it takes seriously the idea that people need to feel understood before they can change. Geist's paper on the forward edge is a beautiful articulation of self psychology's clinical optimism.

Required Readings

  • Berzoff, J., Flanagan, L. M. & Hertz, P. — Ch. 6: The Theory of Self Psychology. Inside Out and Outside In. (26 pgs) request
  • Geist, R. A. (2011). The forward edge, connectedness, and the therapeutic process. Int. J. Psychoanal. Self Psychol., 6, 235–251. (17 pgs) request

Supplemental Readings

  • Newman, K. (2007). Therapeutic action in self psychology. Psychoanal. Quarterly, 76, 1513–1553. (41 pgs)
  • Magid, B., Fosshage, J., & Shane, E. (2021). The emerging paradigm of relational self psychology. Psychoanalysis, Self and Context, 16(1), 1–23. (23 pgs)

Week 11 Clinical Application — Rupture and Repair

Every therapeutic relationship breaks. The question is whether you can repair it—and whether the repair itself becomes the treatment. Muran and Eubanks have done the most rigorous empirical work on alliance ruptures, and their writing is refreshingly jargon-free.

Required Readings

  • Muran, J. C., Eubanks, C. F., & Samstag, L. W. (2021). One more time with less jargon. J. Clinical Psychology, 77, 361–368. (7 pgs) request
  • Eubanks, C. F., Muran, J. C., & Safran, J. D. (2018). Alliance rupture repair: A meta-analysis. Psychotherapy, 55(4), 508–519. (11 pgs) request

Supplemental Readings

  • Friedlander, M. L. (2015). Use of relational strategies to repair alliance ruptures. Psychotherapy, 52(2), 174–179. (6 pgs)

Media

Week 12 The Relational Turn

The relational turn changed everything. It moved psychodynamic therapy away from the analyst-as-blank-screen toward the idea that the therapeutic relationship is a mutual, co-constructed process. Ornstein and Ganzer's paper makes the case for why this matters specifically for social work.

Required Readings

  • Ornstein, E. D. & Ganzer, C. (2005). Relational social work: A model for the future. Families in Society, 86, 565–572. (7 pgs) request
  • Berzoff, J., Flanagan, L. M. & Hertz, P. — Ch. 8: Relational and Intersubjective Theories. Inside Out and Outside In. (16 pgs) request

Supplemental Readings

  • Aron, L. (1991). The patient's experience of the analyst's subjectivity. Psychoanal. Dialogues, 1(1), 29–51. (23 pgs)
  • Bromberg, P. M. (1993). Shadow and substance: A relational perspective on clinical process. Psychoanal. Psychology, 10(2), 147–168. (22 pgs)

Week 13 Intersubjectivity

Intersubjectivity asks the deepest question in psychodynamic theory: what happens between two minds? Ogden's concept of the "analytic third"—the experience that belongs to neither patient nor therapist but is co-created between them—is one of the most profound ideas in clinical work.

Required Readings

  • Quatman, T. — Ch. 7: Understanding, the Bass Clef, and Intersubjectivity. Essential Psychodynamic Psychotherapy. Routledge. request
  • Ogden, T. H. (1994). The analytic third: Working with intersubjective clinical facts. Int. J. Psychoanal., 75, 3–19. (16 pgs) request
  • Bromberg, P. M. (1996). Standing in the spaces: The multiplicity of self and the psychoanalytic relationship. Contemp. Psychoanal., 32, 509–535. (30 pgs) request

Supplemental Readings

  • Arnd-Caddigan, M. (2011). Toward a broader definition of evidence-informed practice: Intersubjective evidence. Families in Society, 92(4), 372–378. (7 pgs)

Media

  • Intersubjectivity. Berlin Psychoanalytic. YouTube. (15 min)
Week 14 From Theory to Practice

We end where the work begins—in the room, with real people, in messy contexts. Ngo-Smith's paper on psychoanalysis and homelessness is a necessary corrective to the fantasy of the idealized consulting room. Ogden's "Ontological Psychoanalysis" asks what kind of clinician you want to become.

Required Readings

  • Ngo-Smith, B. R. (2018). This couch has bed bugs. Clinical Social Work Journal, 46, 26–33. (7 pgs) request
  • Ogden, T. (2019). Ontological psychoanalysis or "What do you want to be when you grow up?" Psychoanal. Quarterly, 88, 661–684. (23 pgs) request

Supplemental Readings

  • Berzoff, J., Flanagan, L. M. & Hertz, P. — Ch. 12: The Bridge: From Theory to Practice. Inside Out and Outside In. (13 pgs)
  • McWilliams, N. — Ch. 12: Self-Care. Psychoanalytic Psychotherapy.
  • Gabbard, G. O. (2000). On gratitude and gratification. J. Amer. Psychoanal. Assn., 48(3), 697–716. (20 pgs)

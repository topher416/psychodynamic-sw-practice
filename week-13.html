Week 13

Intersubjectivity

Intersubjectivity asks the deepest question in psychodynamic theory: what happens between two minds? Ogden’s concept of the “analytic third” — the experience that belongs to neither patient nor therapist but is co-created between them — is one of the most profound ideas in clinical work.

Reflection Prompts

  1. Ogden describes the “analytic third” as an experience that belongs to neither person but emerges between them. Can you recall a session where something seemed to be happening in the room that was not quite yours and not quite the client’s — a mood, a feeling, a sense of something unspoken? How did you work with it, or how might you have?
  2. Bromberg writes about “standing in the spaces” between different self-states. Think about the different versions of yourself that show up in clinical work — the competent one, the uncertain one, the one who wants to rescue, the one who feels helpless. How do you move between them? What happens when a client needs you to be someone you are not sure you can be?
  3. How does intersubjective thinking change the way you understand “objectivity” in clinical assessment? If the clinician’s subjectivity is always part of the data, what does that mean for how you write case notes, present in supervision, or make diagnostic formulations?

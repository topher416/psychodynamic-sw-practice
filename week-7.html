Week 07

Clinical Application — Projection, Splitting, and Projective Identification

If Klein gave us the theory, this week is about what it looks like in practice. Ogden’s paper on projective identification is one of the clearest explanations of a concept that clinicians constantly use but rarely define well. McWilliams grounds it in the language of defense mechanisms that you’ll actually recognize from your caseload.

Reflection Prompts

  1. Think about a time you left a session feeling something that didn’t quite belong to you — an anger, a hopelessness, a sense of incompetence that seemed to come from nowhere. How might Ogden’s model of projective identification help you understand what happened between you and the client in that moment?
  2. McWilliams describes idealization and devaluation as two sides of the same coin. Have you worked with a client who initially put you on a pedestal and then abruptly turned critical or dismissive? How did you make sense of that shift at the time, and how might you understand it differently now through the lens of splitting?
  3. Projection is something every clinician encounters, but it can be difficult to distinguish from accurate perception. When a client says something about you that stings — that you’re not really listening, that you don’t care — how do you discern what belongs to the transference and what might be a legitimate observation about your presence in the room?
